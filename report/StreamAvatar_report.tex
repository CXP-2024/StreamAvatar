\title{StreamAvatar: Real-Time-Capable Audio-Driven Portrait Animation via DyStream Optimization and Blockwise Motion Distillation}

\author{CXP-2024}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The original StreamAvatar proposal aimed to build a real-time audio-driven avatar by composing pretrained multimodal components. During implementation, we found that training a full portrait generator from scratch was unnecessary and unstable for the available time and data. We therefore pivoted to a stronger practical setting: adapting and accelerating DyStream, a pretrained autoregressive flow-matching talking-head model, toward near-real-time streaming inference. This report describes the resulting system. We deploy the DyStream teacher, optimize its inference path, build a browser-facing streaming prototype, and train a blockwise student model that predicts short future motion blocks conditioned on audio, history, and reference anchors. We further study cross-attention and one-step denoising student variants, real-anchor versus synthetic-anchor conditioning, and supervised training from cached LRS3 motion latents. On 60-second examples, the optimized pipeline runs faster than real time on a B200 GPU, while student variants provide roughly 8--11$\times$ faster motion inference than the teacher with acceptable lip synchronization. Our experiments show that real cached anchors give the most stable objective behavior, while mixed noise anchors sometimes improve visible motion strength and long-horizon identity consistency but do not reliably improve SyncNet metrics. The final outcome is not a fully solved avatar generator, but a real-time-capable StreamAvatar prototype with clear empirical lessons about distilling autoregressive flow-matching portrait animation models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%======================================================================

Audio-driven talking portrait generation synthesizes a video of a reference person or avatar speaking according to an input audio stream. It is useful for virtual meetings, low-bandwidth telepresence, privacy-preserving representation, and avatar-based communication. A practical system must satisfy three constraints at the same time: the lips should match the speech, the identity should remain stable over long clips, and inference should be fast enough for interactive use.

Our proposal originally targeted a custom ``StreamAvatar'' model built from pretrained components. Early experiments with FLOAT-style motion latents showed that simple regression can overfit a single clip, but flow-matching training was unstable and hard to scale without more careful priors. We then moved to DyStream~\cite{dystream}, which already contains the important pretrained components: a wav2vec-style audio front-end~\cite{wav2vec2}, an autoregressive GPT-like motion generator, a flow-matching motion sampler~\cite{flowmatching}, and a pretrained renderer. This made the project more realistic: instead of inventing an entire video generator, we optimized and distilled a strong teacher.

This report focuses on that final direction. Our contribution is an engineering and modeling study of real-time-capable audio-driven portrait animation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We deploy DyStream locally and analyze its inference bottlenecks.
    \item We build an optimized and partially streaming inference path with model warm-up, render batching, chunked audio processing, frame queues, and a simple microphone/upload interface.
    \item We train blockwise student models that predict future motion blocks directly from audio, history motion, and reference anchors.
    \item We compare real cached anchors, synthetic anchors, and mixed-anchor training, including PCA/t-SNE distribution analysis.
    \item We evaluate with SyncNet/LSE metrics~\cite{syncnet,wav2lip}, landmark-based mouth motion statistics, loss curves, qualitative videos, and speed measurements.
\end{itemize}

The main result is pragmatic. We do not claim to outperform the teacher in visual quality. Instead, we show that the teacher can be adapted into a real-time-capable system and that a compact blockwise student can substantially reduce motion-generation cost, at the price of some lip-sync and motion fidelity.

DyStream is naturally modular. The model does not generate pixels directly from audio. Instead, audio is encoded into temporal features, an autoregressive motion model predicts a latent motion trajectory, flow matching refines or samples the motion latent, and a pretrained renderer turns the motion into video frames. This is important for real-time work because the expensive pixel-level identity and appearance prior can remain frozen.

In our optimized runtime, the teacher path is used in two ways. First, it is the baseline used for qualitative comparison. Second, it provides pseudo-targets for distillation: for a fixed audio chunk and reference anchor, the teacher produces a motion trajectory, and the student learns to produce a similar trajectory with fewer sequential operations.

\section{Method}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Optimized DyStream Inference}

We first made the DyStream inference path usable as a local application. The important optimizations were not all model-level. Model loading is moved out of the hot path, the reference image is preprocessed once, rendering is batched, and the web app reports stage-level timing. The best practical setting used a small motion chunk size and batched rendering. In one B200 run on a 60-second input, the optimized offline path took about 6.3 seconds total: 3.57 seconds for motion inference, 1.64 seconds for rendering, and 1.02 seconds for muxing. This is a real-time factor of roughly 0.105 for full-file inference.

For the interactive prototype, we added a frame-queue style player. Audio is collected in chunks, processed by the backend, rendered frames are pushed to a queue, and the frontend consumes frames at the target frame rate. We also added a lightweight noise gate for microphone input, because silent microphone noise can drive unwanted mouth motion.

\subsection{Blockwise Student Distillation}

The teacher uses an autoregressive structure. A GPT-like model conditions on previous motion and audio, then the flow-matching module generates motion latents. This is high quality but sequential. Our final student should be understood as a blockwise autoregressive denoising model, not as the teacher's two-stage AR+FM pipeline. For each block, it receives rolling motion history, audio features, a reference anchor, noisy future tokens, and a timestep, then directly predicts a clean future motion block:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{M}}_{t:t+B-1}
    =
    f_\theta(
      \mathbf{A}_{t:t+B-1},
      \mathbf{M}_{t-H:t-1},
      \mathbf{r},
      \mathbf{Z}_{t:t+B-1},
      \tau
    ),
\end{equation}
where $B=2$ in our conservative final student, $H$ is the history length, $\mathbf{A}$ denotes audio features, $\mathbf{r}$ is the reference anchor, $\mathbf{Z}$ denotes noisy future-token input, and $\tau$ is the sampled timestep. During rollout, the predicted block is appended to history and used by the next block.

We explored several student architectures. The most useful version is the blockwise cross-attention denoising student. History tokens and noisy future tokens are processed in one Transformer sequence. Audio and anchor features are injected through cross-attention. The retained ``FM-like'' part is only the noise/timestep interface: during training we create a noisy version of the target future block, and during inference we feed Gaussian future noise at the first scheduler timestep. The student does not run an ODE solver and does not predict a velocity field; it directly predicts clean motion in one forward pass per block.

\subsection{Training Objectives}

The student is trained by rollout. For a training clip, we run the student block by block. Each predicted block is appended to the history for the following block. Gradients are accumulated through the rollout inside one forward/backward step, with optional detachment between blocks for stability. The main loss compares the predicted motion block against either teacher motion or cached real motion:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{motion}} =
    \| \hat{\mathbf{M}} - \mathbf{M}^\star \|_2^2.
\end{equation}
We additionally use velocity, acceleration, and boundary losses:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{motion}}
    + \lambda_v \mathcal{L}_{\text{velocity}}
    + \lambda_a \mathcal{L}_{\text{acceleration}}
    + \lambda_b \mathcal{L}_{\text{boundary}}.
\end{equation}
The velocity and acceleration losses discourage slow-motion collapse and unnatural jitter. The boundary loss makes adjacent predicted blocks connect smoothly.

\subsection{Anchor Conditioning}

The reference anchor is a 512-dimensional motion/identity-style latent extracted from the reference image or cached training video. We tested three anchor regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Real anchor}: use cached real anchors from LRS3 motion latents.
    \item \textbf{Noise anchor}: use synthetic Gaussian or sphere noise anchors.
    \item \textbf{Mixed anchor}: use real anchors for half the batch and synthetic anchors for the other half.
\end{itemize}
The mixed-sphere variant samples
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{r}_{\text{noise}} =
    \bar{\mathbf{r}}_{\text{real}}
    +
    R \cdot
    \frac{\epsilon}{\|\epsilon\|_2},
    \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I),
\end{equation}
with $R=6$. This was intended to cover the real-anchor distribution. However, later PCA analysis showed that high-dimensional sphere sampling does not expand along the true principal directions of real anchors. It is therefore better interpreted as a regularizer than as faithful distribution coverage.

\section{Experiments}
%======================================================================

\subsection{Data and Setup}

We use LRS3~\cite{lrs3} video/audio clips. Two kinds of targets are used. Teacher-distillation targets are generated online by the DyStream teacher. Cached-real targets are generated by preprocessing LRS3 videos into audio waveforms and 512-dimensional motion-latent sequences. For the largest student runs, we used a 60k pretrain-cache manifest with a held-out validation set. Most clips are sampled as 3-second training segments to keep training efficient and compatible with blockwise rollout.

The renderer and teacher components remain frozen. The student is trained with batch size 64 in the main cache/anchor experiments. Validation is run periodically and logged to JSONL. All reported qualitative comparisons use the same audio/reference inputs for teacher and student.

\subsection{Metrics}

We report the following metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LSE-D}: SyncNet audio-video distance; lower is better.
    \item \textbf{LSE-C}: SyncNet confidence; higher is better.
    \item \textbf{Mouth-audio correlation}: correlation between landmark mouth opening and audio energy.
    \item \textbf{Mouth velocity}: average landmark mouth-opening velocity, used as a rough motion-strength indicator.
    \item \textbf{Motion MSE/L1}: latent distance between student and teacher motion.
    \item \textbf{Velocity ratio}: student/teacher motion velocity magnitude. Values closer to one indicate less under-motion.
    \item \textbf{Speedup}: measured student motion rollout speed relative to the teacher rollout in verification.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Speed and Streaming Behavior}

The optimized teacher pipeline already reaches faster-than-real-time throughput for offline 60-second audio on a B200 GPU. The remaining difficulty is not only raw model speed. A real streaming demo also needs warm-up, rolling audio context, rolling motion history, frame buffering, and stable playback. Our current web prototype validates the basic form: models are loaded before inference, audio chunks are processed incrementally, rendered frames are queued, and the frontend plays frames continuously.

The largest remaining gap is interaction latency. Full-file throughput can be much faster than real time, but live streaming latency is bounded by audio chunk duration, backend processing time, render time, and playback buffering. Our current prototype is best described as \textit{real-time-capable} or \textit{near-real-time}, not a fully optimized video-call system.

%======================================================================
\section{Discussion}
%======================================================================

\paragraph{What worked.}
The strongest practical success is the decomposition. Keeping the DyStream renderer and teacher frozen makes the task tractable. Optimizing the runtime gives a fast demo. Distilling motion blocks gives a clear speed-quality trade-off. The blockwise denoising student also avoids the worst failure of plain regression: long-horizon identity collapse and severe slow-motion behavior.

\paragraph{What did not fully work.}
Pure cached-real supervised training from LRS3 motion latents can learn stable motion but may miss behaviors such as natural blinking. This suggests that teacher-generated motion contains useful priors beyond per-frame encoded ground truth. Synthetic anchor training also did not reliably improve objective metrics. It can regularize identity, but simple high-dimensional Gaussian or sphere noise does not match the real-anchor manifold.

\paragraph{Why not simply use one-step teacher FM?}
The original teacher can run with one denoising step, but the AR GPT component remains a bottleneck. Our student attacks that bottleneck by replacing sequential AR+FM rollout with direct blockwise motion prediction. This is why the student can be faster even when the teacher FM itself is already cheap.

\paragraph{Current best recommendation.}
For the final demo and report, the real-anchor student should be treated as the primary student model. The mixed-anchor model should be included as an ablation because it reveals a useful phenomenon: anchor perturbation can affect long-term identity consistency and mouth-motion strength, but naive noise does not consistently improve lip-sync metrics.

%======================================================================
\section{Limitations and Future Work}
%======================================================================

The current evaluation uses a small number of reference images and audio files. A more reliable conclusion needs a multi-speaker benchmark with averaged SyncNet, landmark, identity, and temporal metrics. The current web app is also an experimental prototype. It needs a lower-latency audio transport, stronger front-end frame scheduling, and better handling of browser microphone noise.

Model-wise, the next step is to replace naive synthetic anchors with distribution-aware anchors. A promising direction is PCA/covariance sampling:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{r}_{\text{syn}} =
    \bar{\mathbf{r}} +
    U_k \Sigma_k^{1/2} \epsilon,
\end{equation}
where $U_k$ and $\Sigma_k$ come from real cached anchors. This would sample along the real anchor manifold instead of random 512D directions. Another direction is hybrid teacher/cache training: teacher targets preserve natural motion priors such as blinking, while cached-real targets improve grounding to actual LRS3 data.

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%======================================================================

This project changed from a speculative StreamAvatar proposal into a concrete DyStream-based optimization and distillation study. The final system is a real-time-capable audio-driven portrait animation prototype. We successfully deployed and accelerated the teacher, built a streaming-style app, trained blockwise motion students, and analyzed anchor conditioning. The results show that high-quality pretrained talking-head models can be pushed toward streaming use without training a full renderer, but also that distilling autoregressive flow-matching behavior is not a trivial regression problem. Real anchors are currently the safest training condition; mixed noise anchors are useful as an ablation and regularizer but not a clear improvement. The project therefore provides both a working demo path and a set of lessons for future real-time avatar research.
